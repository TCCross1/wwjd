# Sample day — a fishing household in Capernaum
**Era:** `herod_rome`  
**Book overlay:** Mark 1–2  
**Seats:** a woman grinding; a boat-owner and hired hands; a man at the toll booth  
**Badges:** `from the ground` + `from the text` + `careful guess` on exact tax percentages

This is the weather of the first Gospel, not a Christmas card.

---

## Path

The lake is work.
Before the village is fully awake a woman is already leaning on a grindstone. Bread for a house does not appear.
Men who own a boat and men who only own a back push off together. Someone will take a cut of what they bring in.
The alley will be dust by noon. The synagogue is not far. Rome is not visible, except when it is — a coin, a soldier, a booth.

**This year’s fear:** debt, sickness, and a kingdom that so far looks like other men’s kingdoms.

---

## Trail — dawn to dark

**Dark-blue hour.** Galilee mornings are for work. Nets. The smell of the catch from yesterday if they could keep any. A father and sons, maybe hired hands (Mark 1:20). Not every fisherman is the same class. Owning the boat is one life. Being paid for the night is another.

**The cut.** Fish are food and export (including sauces in the wider lake economy). Rights to take fish are not free in a client kingdom. A booth on the border road — Capernaum sits where Antipas’ Galilee meets other territory — makes empire personal. Levi sits at a *telonion*. People hate the booth because they know the booth.

**Her day.** Two hours can go to grinding enough grain for one small house if it is done by hand. Water. Children. A courtyard shared with kin. Almost no decoration. Mats, not dining sets. Stew and bread. Olives. Fish when the night was kind.

**Heat.** Alleys of packed dirt. Fieldstone rooms. Little imported luxury in this village. Sepphoris and Tiberias, the show cities, are another world a walk away. Two rooms: a plastered dining room up there; a basalt grindstone down here.

**Sabbath, if this is Friday coming on.** Work has an edge. The clock is not only Rome’s.

**Evening.** A house can fill fast — Mark’s Capernaum house does, with the sick at the door. That detail is daily life plus interruption. The village is small enough that a healer is news before sunset.

**Night.** Oil is light you spend. Most talk happens in the dark. A gospel begins as speech in that dark, not as a bound book.

## Pressure map

```
Tiberius (face, title, distant)
  → Pontius Pilate in Judea / Herod Antipas in Galilee
    → local notables, synagogue leaders, temple when you go south
      → toll farmers and harbor dues
        → boat-owner
          → hired hand / woman of the house / child
```

What empire sells as a gift: peace, roads, coins that work in the next town.
What it extracts: produce, dignity, the option of a cross if you become a problem.
What this house fears: a sick child and a debt that eats the boat.

## Converted distances

- Roof of the house to the water: minutes.
- Capernaum to Nazareth: a day’s walk in hill country.
- Capernaum to Jerusalem: several days, climbing into a tense feast city.
- “Take up your cross”: not a necklace. A thing people had seen on a road.

---

## Why this changes how you hear Mark

“Follow me” costs a net that feeds a house.
“Tax collector” is a neighbor with a hated job, not a video-game villain.
“Immediately” in Mark is the pace of a working village that cannot afford a long seminar.
When Jesus touches the unclean, he is crossing a daily boundary, not performing a metaphor.

---

## Path safety

No flogging close-ups.
A cross can be named as “the empire’s way of showing everyone who is in charge.”
A child does not need the tax-rate debate. They need the grindstone and the boat.
