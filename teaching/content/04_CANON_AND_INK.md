# Canon Room, Ink Room, Two Chairs
## House rules for the whole library

These three systems appear in every book. They are how W.W.J.D. earns the name Rosetta Stone.

---

## 1. The Canon Room
*(library home, not inside one book)*

People say “the Bible” as if every community holds the same stack of books. They do not. The app tells that story without picking a fight.

### Shared core
- Jewish Bible (Tanakh): same writings Protestants call the Old Testament, counted and ordered differently (24 books vs. 39).
- All major Christian churches: the same 27-book New Testament.

### Where the stacks differ
| Collection | Old Testament / first testament | Total commonly cited |
|---|---|---|
| Jewish Tanakh | Torah, Prophets, Writings | 24 books (same material as Protestant OT, different grouping) |
| Protestant | 39 OT books | 66 |
| Catholic | 46 OT books (includes deuterocanon) | 73 |
| Eastern Orthodox | broader Septuagint tradition | often 76–77 |
| Ethiopian Orthodox | still broader | often described near 81 |

### The seven books Catholics call deuterocanonical
Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, 1 Maccabees, 2 Maccabees, plus Greek additions inside Esther and Daniel.

Protestants usually call these the Apocrypha: valuable to many for history and devotion, not used to settle doctrine.
Catholics and Orthodox receive them as Scripture. Orthodox lists add still more (3 Maccabees, Psalm 151, 1 Esdras, Prayer of Manasseh, and others depending on church).

### Why the split exists (both sides at full strength)

**Chair A — shorter Hebrew canon (Jewish and most Protestant)**  
The books Jesus’ Jewish world treated as the core Hebrew Scriptures track with the later rabbinic collection. Reformers returned to that list. These extra books were written later, mostly in Greek, and were disputed even in some early Christian lists.

**Chair B — wider Greek canon (Catholic and Orthodox)**  
The first Christians often read the Septuagint, a Greek collection that already included these books. The Church prayed and taught with them for centuries. “Deuterocanonical” means they were received after more debate, not that they are second-class.

**Shared ground**  
Nobody is inventing a 21st-century surprise list. The argument is about which ancient collection the Church should treat as binding Scripture.

**App default**  
Build the 66-book Protestant path first because that is the most common English-app expectation.
Put an **Other Shelves** door on the library home so Catholic and Orthodox users are not erased.
Never call the extra books “fake.” Call them what each tradition calls them.

## 2. The Ink Room
*(how words got from a scroll to a phone)*

### Old Testament witnesses a user should actually meet

**Masoretic Text (MT)**  
Hebrew text preserved with extraordinary care by Jewish scholars called Masoretes (roughly 7th–10th centuries AD), using a consonantal text that is much older. Almost every modern Protestant Old Testament starts here (especially the Leningrad Codex, AD 1008).

**Septuagint (LXX)**  
Greek translation begun in Alexandria in the 3rd century BC. This was the Bible of many early Christians. New Testament writers often quote Greek wording. Sometimes it is shorter or longer than the MT (Jeremiah is the famous case). Sometimes it preserves an older Hebrew reading.

**Dead Sea Scrolls (DSS)**  
Hebrew and Aramaic copies from about the 3rd century BC to the 1st century AD, found beginning in 1947. They showed that Jewish communities already had more than one form of some books. Sometimes a scroll agrees with the MT. Sometimes it agrees with the Greek. Sometimes it goes its own way.

**Samaritan Pentateuch**  
The first five books as kept by the Samaritan community. Sometimes useful when the question is “what did an ancient copy of Moses’ books look like?”

### New Testament witnesses a user should actually meet

**Modern critical Greek text** (Nestle-Aland / UBS families)  
Built by comparing thousands of manuscripts and preferring earlier and more independent witnesses. Basis of ESV, NIV, NRSV, NASB, CSB, NLT, and most modern Bibles.

**Byzantine / Majority text**  
The form of the Greek text copied most often in the medieval church.

**Textus Receptus**  
Printed Greek New Testaments of the 1500s–1600s, close cousins of the Byzantine stream. Basis of the King James and New King James New Testaments.

### Translation families (speak in families, not brand wars)

| Family | Examples | Feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Older English church stream | KJV, NKJV | Elevated, familiar to many American ears |
| Essentially literal modern | ESV, NASB, CSB | Close to the Greek/Hebrew sentence |
| Mediating / meaning-driven | NIV, NLT, GNT | Smooth English, more “what it means” |
| Academic / church-wide | NRSV, CEB | Often notes other witnesses in footnotes |

Rule for the app: when a word fight matters, show 2–3 English renderings and the underlying word. When it does not change anything a disciple would do, stay quiet.

### Famous Ink cases the library should be ready to teach
- Isaiah 7:14 — Hebrew *almah* (“young woman”) / Greek *parthenos* (“virgin”)
- Genesis 1:1–2 — “in the beginning God created” vs. “when God began to create”
- John 1:1 — “the Word was God” vs. fringe “a god” arguments, explained fairly and firmly from Greek grammar
- Mark 16:9–20 and John 7:53–8:11 — later-attested endings / stories, with both pastoral and textual chairs
- 1 John 5:7 in the KJV — Comma Johanneum, a late Trinitarian sentence not in early Greek copies
- Deuteronomy 32:8 — “sons of Israel” (MT) vs. “sons of God” (LXX + some DSS)

## 3. Two Chairs — how to write a fork

A chair is allowed only if all of these are true:
1. Real people who love Scripture (or study it at a high level) sit there.
2. They have texts, not only vibes.
3. The difference changes how someone reads the book, not just a footnote hobby.

Template the writer must fill:

**The question in one human sentence.**  
**Chair A name** — who sits here — their best case — their texts.  
**Chair B name** — who sits here — their best case — their texts.  
**What both still share.**  
**What shouting will not settle.**

Never add a cartoon third chair (“people who hate God”) unless a user asks about a fringe reading. Then label it fringe.

### Chair types that show up often
- Authorship (Moses / many hands; Paul / a student of Paul)
- Dating (close to the events / generations later)
- Genre (history / liturgy / parable / vision)
- How to read origins (Genesis 1–11)
- How to read future (Revelation)
- How a law or a household code lands after Jesus

The app does not issue a winner badge.
The WWJD companion, already built, may still say “here is the way Jesus’ own pattern leans” without pretending the historical debate is over.
