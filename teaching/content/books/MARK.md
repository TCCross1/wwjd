# Mark
**Section:** Gospel  
**Genre:** Gospel (announcement-story of Jesus, not a modern biography)  
**Default map:** The land in Jesus’ days + Jerusalem close-up

Mark is the gold-master for “how to read a Gospel.”

---

## 1. The Door

**One sentence:** Mark is a fast, spare Gospel that runs toward a cross and asks, in the dark, whether you can see a Son of God there.

**Image-idea:** A dusty road. A man walking ahead of his friends. They are afraid. He does not slow down.

**Time ribbon:** First generation after Jesus. Rome is the air everyone breathes.

**Path line:** “This is the shortest Gospel. Jesus heals, teaches, is killed, and rises. The ending sends you back to Galilee.”

---

## 2. The Campfire

### Path
Mark does not start with a baby in a manger. A wild preacher named John appears in the wilderness. Jesus is baptized, tempted, and then moves fast — “immediately” is one of Mark’s favorite words. He calls fishermen. He drives out what ruins people. Crowds come. Religious leaders bristle. His own family worries. He tells seed-stories. He feeds hungry people. He walks on the water. Three times He tells His friends He will be rejected and killed and rise. They do not get it. Peter says He is the Christ, then tries to talk Him out of suffering. In Jerusalem He clears the temple court, eats a last meal, prays in a garden, is arrested, denied, tried, mocked, and crucified. A Roman officer says, “This man was the Son of God.” Women watch. On Sunday they find the tomb open. A messenger says He has gone ahead to Galilee. In the oldest ending we have, the women run, trembling, and say nothing — yet. The story leaves the next step in the reader’s hands.

### Trail
Mark is written to be heard in a sitting. It feels like breath after running. Titles collide on purpose: Jesus is Messiah and Servant, strong and silent, recognized by demons before He is recognized by disciples.

A likely first audience knew what Rome could do to bodies. Mark does not hide the shame of the cross. He puts it in the center and dares you to call that man King.

There is a long ending (16:9–20) in many Bibles. There is also a short, abrupt ending at 16:8 in the earliest Greek copies. The Ink room must tell that story without panicking a new believer and without pretending the verses were always there.

## 3. The Passport

**Traditional author:** John Mark, helper of Peter and Paul, writing Peter’s preaching in Rome.  
**Why people hold this:** Early church memory (Papias and others) links Mark to Peter. The book has Peter’s fingerprints: his failures are not sanded off.

**Other serious proposals:** An anonymous Jesus-follower in a Roman or Syrian church, using living oral tradition, writing around the shock of the temple’s destruction or the Neronian persecutions. The name “Mark” is attached early and may still be right.  
**Why people hold this:** The Gospel never signs its name. Modern study starts from that fact.

**Traditional date:** Often the 50s–60s AD, while Peter is still alive or just after his death.  
**Academic range:** Commonly c. 65–75 AD, with many landing near the Jewish War (66–73) and the fall of the temple in 70.  
**Why dates differ:** Chapter 13 can be read as Jesus foretelling the temple’s fall, or as a writer already standing in that smoke.

**Setting of the story:** Galilee, the road, then Jerusalem.  
**Likely first audience:** Gentile and Jewish believers under pressure — Rome is a strong traditional guess.  
**Language:** Greek, with Aramaic words left in place (*Talitha koum*, *Eloi Eloi lema sabachthani*) as if we should still hear Jesus’ mother tongue.  
**Purpose in one line:** To announce the good news of Jesus the Messiah, Son of God, whose idea of power is a cross and an empty tomb.

---

## 4. The World Around the Words

- Rome’s peace was real and expensive. Crucifixion was public messaging: this is what happens to threats.
- Galilee was mixed, watched, and alive with hope that God would soon put the world right.
- “Messiah” meant different things — warrior, priest, hidden son of David. Mark’s Jesus keeps breaking the script.
- Synagogue on Sabbath, fishing at night, purity rules, temple tax, a widow’s coins: the book is street-level.
- If Mark writes near Nero or near 70 AD, then “take up your cross” is not a metaphor first. It is a picture people had seen.

## 5. The Map

Pins (certainty mostly **anchored** unless noted):
- Nazareth, wilderness of Judea, Jordan River
- Capernaum — Jesus’ Galilean base
- Sea of Galilee — storms, crossings, feedings
- Gerasa / “country of the Gerasenes” — **disputed** exact town; east side of the lake
- Tyre and Sidon — a step beyond Israel’s house
- Caesarea Philippi — Peter’s confession, near the headwaters of the Jordan
- Jericho — Bartimaeus
- Bethphage, Bethany, Mount of Olives, Jerusalem, Golgotha, the tomb

### On the tray
**Find:** Lead net-weight; first-century Galilee working boat (type); Capernaum basalt courtyard house.  
**Feel:** “Follow me” costs a night’s work. The village is stone and courtyard, not marble.  
**Does not prove:** This hull or this house was Peter’s.  
**Badge:** excavated · type-object

Movement: Galilee (power and misunderstanding) → the road south (three passion predictions) → Jerusalem week.

## 6. How to Read This Kind of Book

**This genre does:** Proclaim a person. Scenes are chosen so you will trust, follow, and not mistake Jesus for a mascot.  
**This genre does not:** Give a school-year calendar of every day of His life. John and Mark are allowed to arrange material for meaning.

Rules:
1. Read it out loud if you can. Mark is paced for ears.
2. Watch the disciples fail. That is pastoral, not slander.
3. Do not skip the silence of chapter 15. The cross is the loudest theological sentence Mark writes.

**Common misuse:** Treating Mark as the “simple Gospel for beginners” and missing how hard it is. Or flattening Jesus into only wonder-worker or only victim.

---

## 7. The Walkthrough

1. **The opening bell** — 1:1–15 — John, baptism, wilderness, “the kingdom has drawn near.”
2. **Authority in Galilee** — 1:16–3 — calling, healing, conflict over Sabbath and mercy.
3. **Parables and power** — 4–6 — seeds, storm, the dead raised, a hometown that will not believe.
4. **Bread and blindness** — 6–8 — feedings, a Gentile mother’s faith, a half-healed blind man who becomes a picture of the disciples.
5. **The hinge** — 8:27–10 — “You are the Christ.” Then the suffering Son of Man. Transfiguration. A rich man walks away. A blind beggar sees.
6. **The week in the city** — 11–13 — donkey, temple, arguments, a widow, a long warning.
7. **Night and noon** — 14–15 — meal, garden, trial, cross, the temple curtain, a Gentile confession.
8. **Morning** — 16 — open tomb. Women. Galilee promised. Then the question of the ending.

---

## 8. The People

- **Jesus** — moving ahead. Teaching in action more than in long speeches.
- **Peter, James, John** — inner three; still capable of sleep and panic.
- **The Twelve** — named, sent, and often confused.
- **The women** — they watch the death and they come to the tomb. Mark trusts them with the first news.
- **The authorities** — temple leadership and Rome, different fears, one cross.
- **Bartimaeus** — the man who sees and follows on the road.

---

## 9. Two Chairs

### Chair set 1 — When was it written, and does chapter 13 look back at 70 AD?
**Chair A:** Jesus foresaw the temple’s fall. Mark writes the warning down before or as it happens.  
**Chair B:** Mark writes in the generation of the war and shapes chapter 13 so a bleeding church can endure.  
**Shared ground:** The temple did fall. Mark wants disciples awake, not date-setters.

### Chair set 2 — The ending of Mark
**Question:** Did Mark mean to stop at 16:8, or has the original ending been lost, or is 16:9–20 original?

**Chair A — Stop at 16:8.** The oldest complete Greek copies (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) end with the women afraid. The abruptness is the point: go to Galilee and meet Him yourself.

**Chair B — Longer ending belongs in the church’s Bible.** Most later manuscripts and many centuries of reading include 16:9–20. It matches appearances the other Gospels tell.

**A live third:** The long ending is early church summary, precious as testimony, not Mark’s first last line.

**Shared ground:** Mark’s Jesus rises. The women are first. Galilee is the meeting place. The other Gospels are not silent.  
**Not settled by:** Ripping pages out or pretending footnotes do not exist.

---

## 10. The Ink

**Mark 1:1 — “Son of God”**  
Some early copies have the phrase, some do not. The title still detonates at the baptism, the transfiguration, and the cross.

**Mark 16:9–20**  
Missing from the earliest complete manuscripts; present in the majority of later ones. A shorter “they reported to Peter…” ending also exists.  
Good app behavior: print 16:8 as Mark’s oldest full stop, print 16:9–20 as a clearly marked early appendix the church has long read, and refuse to treat either readers of the short ending or readers of the long ending as enemies of the Gospel.

**Aramaic left untranslated at first**  
*Talitha koum* — “Little girl, get up.”  
*Eloi Eloi lema sabachthani* — the psalm on the cross.  
Mark wants you to hear a Jewish man dying, not a myth floating above Israel.

---

## 11. Hard Ground

- **“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”** Psalm 22 on His lips. Pastoral chair: the Son goes into our God-forsakenness. Literary chair: the psalm that starts in godforsakenness ends in worldwide worship — Mark expects you to know the rest of the song. Teach both.
- **Jesus telling people not to talk about Him.** The so-called messianic secret. He will not be owned as a mascot-king.
- **Passages used against people with illness or disability.** Mark’s healings are compassion and kingdom, not a test that faith always equals a cured body this week.

---

## 12. The Thread to Jesus

This whole book *is* the thread. Do not allegorize Mark into something else. Let the man in the road be the answer to WWJD. He touches the unclean. He eats with the wrong table. He refuses to perform for skeptics. He walks toward Jerusalem anyway. He is silent when silence is strength and loud when a house of prayer has become a market. He dies with a psalm in His mouth. He does not stay dead.

**Following Him:** The disciple in Mark is the one who gets up and takes the Galilee road after failure.

**Remember**
1. Mark is in a hurry toward a cross, not toward a brand.
2. The people closest to Jesus can still miss Him.
3. Fear at the tomb is not the last word — even if a manuscript stops there.

**Practice**  
Read Mark 8:27–38 without skipping. Ask one question only: what would I try to talk Jesus out of?
