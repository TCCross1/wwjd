# The whole library
## Who received which books, who set which books aside, and why

This is the Canon Room for the teaching layer.
It must stay two-chair honest. Nobody “stole books from the Bible” in a cartoon. Communities drew different lines around a shared core, at different times, for reasons they can name.

---

## The shared core

Almost every synagogue and church agrees on this stack:

- The books Protestants call the 39-book Old Testament (Jews count the same writings as 24 books: Torah, Prophets, Writings).
- The 27-book New Testament.

That core is not a modern compromise. It is the overlap of living communities.

Everything else in this file is about the shelves around that core.

---

## How to speak in the app

| Community says | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Tanakh / Hebrew Bible** | Jewish collection. Same writings as the Protestant OT, different order and grouping. |
| **Protocanon** | Books never seriously doubted in the Great Church. |
| **Deuterocanon** | Catholic/Orthodox word: received after more debate, not “second-class.” |
| **Apocrypha** | Protestant word for the same extra OT books: useful to many, not used to settle doctrine. |
| **Pseudepigrapha** | Modern academic label for books written under an ancient name (Enoch, Jubilees, 4 Ezra). Not an ancient insult. |
| **Narrower / broader Tewahedo canon** | Western scholars’ way of describing Ethiopian lists. Ethiopia itself speaks of 81 books and a living tradition, not always of those two English adjectives. |

Never say “the original Bible had 88 books and Rome deleted them.”
Never say “Ethiopia invented extra books last week.”
Both sentences are false.

## Map of the stacks

| Tradition | Typical count | What is extra vs Protestant 66 |
|---|---|---|
| Jewish Tanakh | 24 books (same material as 39) | No NT. No deuterocanon as Scripture. |
| Protestant | 66 | — |
| Catholic | 73 | Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees, Greek additions to Esther and Daniel |
| Eastern Orthodox | ~76–77 | Catholic extras + 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151, Prayer of Manasseh; some lists add 4 Maccabees as appendix |
| Ethiopian / Eritrean Tewahedo | 81 (counting varies) | Most of the above (not Greek 1–2 Maccabees) **plus** 1 Enoch, Jubilees, 1–3 Meqabyan, 4 Baruch, 4 Ezra, and in the broader reckoning church-order books |

The New Testament 27 is shared.
Ethiopia’s “35 NT books” in some counts is the 27 plus church-order works (Sinodos, Books of the Covenant, Ethiopic Clement, Didascalia) — not extra Gospels.

## The Ethiopian / Eritrean collection

Christianity reached the Aksumite kingdom in the fourth century, in conversation with Alexandria. Ethiopia was then isolated from the later Mediterranean councils that narrowed many lists. It kept a wider Second Temple and Alexandrian inheritance, copied in Ge’ez.

### Books that especially mark this shelf

**1 Enoch (Mäṣḥafä Henok)**  
A Jewish apocalyptic collection. Oldest parts (Book of the Watchers, Astronomical Book) reach the 3rd–4th centuries BC. Complete book survives in Ge’ez. Aramaic fragments at Qumran. Jude 14–15 quotes Enoch 1:9. Ethiopia reads it as Scripture. Almost every other church does not.

**Jubilees (Mäṣḥafä Kufale)**  
A Hebrew retelling of Genesis–Exodus in jubilee-years, stressing calendar, covenant, and angels. Written mid-2nd century BC. ~14 Hebrew copies at Qumran — more copies than many books everyone calls “Bible.” Complete text survives in Ge’ez.

**1–3 Meqabyan**  
Ethiopian works. **Not** the Greek 1–4 Maccabees. They share a name and a concern with faithfulness under pagan pressure. Confusing them with the Catholic Maccabees is the most common Western error about this canon.

**4 Baruch (Paralipomena of Jeremiah / Teref Baruch)**  
A sequel-story to Jeremiah set around the fall of Jerusalem. Known in Greek and other languages; canonical in the Tewahedo OT.

**4 Ezra (Ezra Sutuel)**  
A Jewish apocalypse after 70 AD, kept in Latin Bibles as 2 Esdras chs. 3–14, and in Ethiopia as Scripture.

**Broader-canon church books** (often listed to reach or fill the number 81): Josippon (medieval Jewish history received in Ge’ez), Sinodos (four church-order collections), two Books of the Covenant, Ethiopic Clement (not the same as 1–2 Clement), Ethiopic Didascalia.

Honesty required in the UI: the number 81 is traditional (Fetha Nagast). Itemized lists do not always add the same way. Some books are hard to find even inside Ethiopia. Do not pretend there is one printed “Ethiopian Bible” on every shelf that looks like a Western brick of 81 labeled sections.

## Who drew which line — a timeline without propaganda

### Second Temple Judaism (before and around Jesus)
There was a widely honored core: Torah first, then Prophets. The Writings were slower to sit still. Sirach’s grandson (late 2nd century BC) already speaks of Law, Prophets, and other writings. Qumran copied Genesis, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, and Psalms in stacks — and also Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit. “Bible” as a closed table of contents is the wrong picture for 100 BC.

### Josephus, late first century AD
He speaks of 22 sacred Jewish books, long fixed, not a wild library. That number matches the later 24/39 grouping if Ruth is with Judges and Lamentations with Jeremiah. He is describing a Jewish consensus in his circle, not a vote at a press conference.

### “Council of Jamnia” (~90 AD) — the textbook story and the better story
Older textbooks said rabbis at Yavneh/Jamnia closed the Hebrew canon and dumped the Apocrypha.
Better current history: rabbis at Yavneh debated *how to use* some Writings (Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes). They did not sit as a Bible-publishing board. The Hebrew collection tightened across the first centuries after the Temple fell. The old Jamnia story is too neat.

### Why many Jews did not keep the extra Greek books
- They were often later, and often Greek (or survived mainly in Greek).
- After 70 AD, a people without a temple leaned harder on a Hebrew core.
- Apocalyptic books had fed hope and revolt. After failed wars, many teachers cooled on them.
- That is a historical reason, not a slur.

### The first churches
They inherited Jewish Scripture, often in Greek (Septuagint). That Greek collection already included books not in the later rabbinic list. New Testament writers quote the Greek wording of the OT constantly. Jude uses Enoch. Hebrews and other books breathe the same air as Wisdom and Maccabees without always naming them.

Early lists disagree at the edges:
- Muratorian Fragment (late 2nd / early 3rd, dates debated): NT mostly in view; Shepherd of Hermas “to be read,” not in the prophets.
- Origen, Melito, Cyril, Athanasius: OT lists closer to the Hebrew 22, with extra books often called useful.
- Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (AD 367): first surviving list that matches the exact 27 NT books. His OT follows the 22 and sets Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and the like in a second rank — “read,” not “canon.”
- Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397, 419): Western lists that include the deuterocanonical books later confirmed by Trent.
- East: Revelation was doubted in some Greek churches for centuries. The 27 still won.

### Jerome (late 300s–400s)
He translated from Hebrew and said the Church reads the extra books but should not build dogma on them. The Latin church kept reading them anyway. This tension is the seed of the later Protestant move.

### Ethiopia and the Coptic world
They were not in the room at Hippo, Carthage, or Trent. They kept Enoch and Jubilees because their fathers had copied them and their liturgy had room for them. Isolation after the Council of Chalcedon (451) meant they were not pressured to match a Roman or Constantinopolitan table of contents.

### Reformation (1500s)
Luther, Calvin, and the churches that followed them went back to the Hebrew 39 for the OT. Luther translated the extra books and parked them in an “Apocrypha” section — “useful to read, not to found doctrine.” English Protestant Bibles often kept that section into the 1800s, then dropped it from popular printings to save paper and to make a point.
Reasons they named:
- These books were not in the Hebrew canon Jesus’ synagogue used as its core.
- Some teachings (prayer for the dead in 2 Maccabees 12) were being used for doctrines they rejected.
- Some books felt later and less prophetic.

### Council of Trent, 1546
Rome dogmatically defined the Catholic 73. That was in part a reply to the Reformers. Catholics do not experience this as “adding books in 1546.” They experience it as naming what the Latin church had been reading.

### Eastern Orthodox churches
They never sat at Trent. They kept a Septuagint-shaped OT. Exact extras vary by church. 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, Baruch are normal. 3 Maccabees, 1 Esdras, Psalm 151, Prayer of Manasseh commonly join them.

## Book-by-book: who does not carry it, and the reasons they give

### Deuterocanon / Protestant Apocrypha

**Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch + Letter of Jeremiah, 1–2 Maccabees, additions to Esther and Daniel**

| Who receives | Who sets aside | Reasons given for setting aside | Reasons given for keeping |
|---|---|---|---|
| Catholic, Orthodox, Tewahedo (Tewahedo keeps most; uses Meqabyan instead of Greek Maccabees) | Jews; Protestants as dogma | Not in the Hebrew collection; some late / Greek; 2 Maccabees used for purgatory / prayer for the dead | In the Septuagint the first Christians prayed with; cited by fathers; Trent and Orthodox liturgy |

**1 Esdras (3 Ezra), 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151, Prayer of Manasseh, 4 Maccabees (appendix)**  
Mostly Orthodox / some Tewahedo. Protestants: not Hebrew canon. Catholics: not Trent’s list (Prayer of Manasseh often printed with the Vulgate appendix).

### Ethiopian-distinct

**1 Enoch**  
Kept: Tewahedo.  
Set aside by: rabbinic Judaism; Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant canons.  
Reasons for setting aside: not in Hebrew canon or LXX core lists; composite and late in parts; Watchers story seemed to shift blame for evil onto angels; 4th-century fathers (Jerome, Augustine, Hilary) cooled; Hippo/Carthage/Trent never listed it.  
Reasons for keeping: Jude quotes it; early fathers used it; Qumran copied it; Ethiopia never stopped.

**Jubilees**  
Kept: Tewahedo.  
Set aside by: almost everyone else.  
Reasons against: not in Josephus’ 22; 2nd-century BC date; calendar polemic; not in Western/Eastern conciliar lists.  
Reasons for: heavy Qumran presence; retells Genesis as covenant instruction; Ethiopia received it with the Law story.

**1–3 Meqabyan**  
Kept only in Tewahedo. Unknown as Scripture elsewhere because they are Ethiopian compositions, not the Greek Maccabees.

**4 Baruch**  
Tewahedo OT. Elsewhere: interesting, not canon.

**4 Ezra**  
Tewahedo; also in some Slavonic/Latin appendixes. Protestants: apocrypha/appendix. Catholics: not Trent dogma (Vulgate appendix).

### New Testament books that were *almost* left out

These made the 27, but the fight is part of the Rosetta Stone:

| Book | Who hesitated | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrews | Some Western voices | Anonymous; style not obviously Paul |
| James | Luther called it an “epistle of straw” | Seemed to fight Paul on faith and works |
| 2 Peter | Many modern scholars still doubt Petrine authorship | Style; late feel |
| 2–3 John, Jude | Size; Jude’s use of Enoch |
| Revelation | Some Eastern churches for centuries | Millennial fights; hard visions |

Athanasius in 367 already has the 27. That did not end every local hesitation.

### Books some early Christians loved and the Great Church did not canonize

Shepherd of Hermas, Didache, 1 Clement, Epistle of Barnabas, Gospel of Peter, Apocalypse of Peter.  
Sinaiticus even binds Barnabas and Hermas after Revelation.  
“In an early Christian codex” ≠ “in the final canon.” Teach that distinction. Ethiopia’s broader NT church-order books are the living cousin of this older wider reading-list.

## Two chairs the whole app should keep in view

**Chair A — shorter line (Jewish / Protestant instinct)**  
God’s people can recognize a tighter prophetic core. Later edifying books are not the same thing as the Law, the Prophets, and the apostolic witness. A clear line protects the gospel from being settled by a useful story.

**Chair B — wider line (Catholic / Orthodox / Tewahedo instinct)**  
The Church did not receive a PDF from heaven. It received a living library in Greek and in worship. Books the saints prayed with, and that the Spirit used, belong in the bound book. Ethiopia is not eccentric; it is a church that was not told to throw Enoch in the river.

**Shared ground**  
Jesus and the apostles live inside Israel’s Scriptures. The 27-book NT is the church’s common apostolic witness. No living church treats the Gospel of Thomas as Scripture. The argument is about the *edges of the first testament* and a few Ethiopian church-order books — not about whether Matthew is in.

**What shouting will not settle**  
A Facebook post that says “they hid Enoch because of the giants.”
A Facebook post that says “Ethiopia is a cult for reading Enoch.”

## How W.W.J.D. should house this

- Default path: 66, because that is the common English expectation.
- Always-visible **Other Shelves**: Catholic/Orthodox deuterocanon, then Tewahedo distinct, then broader church-order.
- Every extra book wears a badge: `Catholic+Orthodox` / `Orthodox` / `Tewahedo` / `Tewahedo broader`.
- Every extra book’s Passport includes a short **“Who set this aside, and what they said.”**
- Never use “fake.” Use “not received as canon by ___.”
