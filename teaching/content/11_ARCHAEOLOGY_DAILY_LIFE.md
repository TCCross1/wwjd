# Archaeological evidence for daily life
## What is in the ground, what we may put on the object tray, what we must not claim

This file is the evidence layer under the World Engine (`10_WORLD_ENGINE.md`).
It exists so the app never paints a pretty village that archaeology cannot support — and never hides the finds that make a book’s weather real.

Rule of voice: **the dirt first, the Bible second, the guess last.**  
A find can rhyme with a verse without “proving the verse.” A missing find does not disprove a verse. Say that out loud.

---

## How to badge every object in the app

| Badge | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `excavated` | recovered in a documented dig, dated by context or style |
| `inscription` | writing from the period naming people, places, rations, taxes, gods |
| `neighbor-text` | Egyptian, Babylonian, Persian, Greek, or Roman record |
| `type-object` | this class of thing is common; this exact piece did not belong to the biblical person |
| `debated` | serious archaeologists disagree about identity or date |
| `not found` | we do not have this, and silence is not a verdict |

Never label a tourist replica “Peter’s house proven.”
Never label a type-object “the very net of Andrew.”

## 1. Iron Age Israel and Judah (roughly 1200–586 BC)
**Books that drink this weather:** Judges, Ruth, Samuel, Kings, early prophets, parts of Torah as lived later.

### Houses
Archaeologists recovered a repeating house plan across the highlands and Judah: the **four-room / pillared house**. Entrance into a long space, side rooms often used for storage and animals, a broad room at the back, often a second story for sleeping. Stone lower courses, mud-brick upper walls, flat roofs.

Worked examples:
- **Tell Halif**, 8th century BC (destroyed in the Assyrian wave of 701). K8 and A8 houses: grindstone set in a built installation, loom weights, lamps, ovens. Activity areas can be mapped — grinding and weaving in the same working rooms.
- **Tel ʿEton**, Building 101: smashed storage jars still holding olive pits, grape stones, lentils, bitter vetch; wheat on the floor; 41 loom weights in one room.
- Farmhouse near **Tel Beit Mirsim**, late 7th–early 6th century: four-room plan, silo, cisterns, agricultural installations by a road.

What this lets the app say: a typical house was a workshop and a barn and a bedroom. Bread and cloth were made at home. A “family” in the text often matches a roof, not a modern apartment.

What this does not prove: that every Israelite house looked identical, or that the plan is a secret theology diagram (some scholars argue it is; that is Summit, not Path).

### Food and work
From floors and jars: barley, wheat, olives, grapes, lentils. Sheep and goat dominate the animal bone picture. Grindstones and pounders are everywhere — Halif published dozens of grindstones and multiple ovens in one town horizon.

App line: *breakfast is labor.* The grindstone is the first object on the Iron Age tray.

### Power that leaves a scar
Halif is one of the Judahite towns wrecked in 701 BC when Sennacherib came south after Samaria had already fallen. The burned house is how “Assyria” feels. It is not a logo.

### Writing in a crisis
**Lachish ostraca** (ink on broken pottery), found in the gate area of the level destroyed by Babylon in 588/586 BC. An officer writes to his lord Ya’ush. Blessing in the name of YHWH. Self-lowering language (“who is your servant, a dog”). Fire-signals: “we are watching for the signals of Lachish… we do not see Azekah.” That last line sits next to Jeremiah 34:7, which names Lachish and Azekah as the last fortified cities standing.

App use: this is daily military literacy, panic, and a prophet’s world in the same decade. It is not Jeremiah’s autograph.

### Oldest biblical wording
**Ketef Hinnom** silver amulets, late 7th / early 6th century BC, Jerusalem tomb. A form of the priestly blessing (Numbers 6). Not a book. A blessing worn on a body. Oldest biblical words we have.

## 2. Egypt: brick, quotas, Semites
**Books:** Exodus 1–15.

### What is solid
- **Tomb of Rekhmire**, Thebes, ~15th century BC: painted brickyard. Workers (Egyptian, Nubian, Syrian/Semitic) draw water, mix mud, strike bricks in a wooden mold, carry them up a ramp. An overseer is in the picture. This is the visual for the object tray: mold, pool, yoke of bricks.
- **Ramesside texts** (leather scroll of Ramesses II’s year 5; Papyri Anastasi III–V): brick *quotas* issued to junior officers (one text: 2,000 bricks per stable-master). Other lines complain there are **no men to make bricks and no straw in the district**. That rhyme with Exodus 5 is real and should be labeled `neighbor-text`, not “the missing page of Exodus.”
- **Pi-Ramesses** (Qantir) and Delta store-city architecture: a vast Ramesside capital of brick palaces, barracks, and precincts — the kind of building program Exodus 1:11 names when it says Pithom and Raamses.
- **Tell el-Dabʿa / Avaris**: centuries of Asiatic / Canaanite material culture in the eastern Delta (houses, burials, pottery). Semites lived in Egypt. That is not debated.
- **Brooklyn Papyrus 35.1446** (~18th–17th c. BC): a household slave list with many West Semitic names, including a woman’s name read as **Shiphra**. Suggestive. Not a photograph of Exodus 1:15.

### What is not solid
- No inscription that says “the Hebrews of Moses made these bricks.”
- The Pharaoh of the oppression is not named in Exodus and is not identified on a stone we can point to without a fight.
- **Apiru / Habiru = Hebrews** is `debated`. Do not print it as a solved equation.
- Quotas and missing straw show the *system* Exodus describes. They do not date the exodus by themselves.

App honesty: show Rekhmire’s yard and the quota texts as the weather. Leave the passport chairs for the date.

## 3. Exile: two rooms we can actually document
**Books:** 2 Kings 24–25, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Psalm 137.

### Room A — a king on a ration list
German excavations at Babylon (Koldewey, 1899–1917) recovered palace archive tablets published by Weidner. They issue oil to **Ia-’-ú-kin, king of the land of Yahud**, and to his sons, among other foreign captives. Dates fall in Nebuchadnezzar’s reign, after 597 BC.

This is as close as archaeology comes to a named biblical person in daily administration. 2 Kings 25:27–30 says the exiled king was later given a regular allowance. The tablets show the allowance world already running.

Object tray: a cuneiform tablet. Caption: *oil for a king who still has a title and no throne.*

### Room B — Judah-town
The **Āl-Yāhūdu / “Judah-town” tablets** (published collections; many from the antiquities market, so find-spot is not a clean grid). Dates run ~572–477 BC — from a generation after the first deportation into the Persian years.

They show:
- a settlement named after Judah in the Babylonian countryside, likely near Nippur / the canal country Ezekiel names
- Judeans in the **land-for-service** system: a plot of barley or dates in exchange for tax and labor
- promissory notes, sureties, witnesses
- Yahwistic names (`-Yama` / `-Yawa` = YHWH) standing in Akkadian contracts
- at least one tablet with paleo-Hebrew letters on the edge

This is the ordinary exile: not only harps on willows, also debt in dates.

**Limit:** because many tablets were not excavated in place, the exact pin on the map is a best guess. Say so.

## 4. Persian diaspora besides Babylon
**Books:** Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther’s world, the “how do we stay a people” question.

**Elephantine papyri** (5th century BC, island in the Nile at Egypt’s southern frontier). A Jewish military colony under Persia.

Daily life in ink:
- marriage contracts, house sales, inheritance
- Ananiah, a temple official, marries Tamut, an Egyptian slave; later emancipation; a house across from their temple
- women who can hold and inherit property (Mibtahiah’s archive)
- a letter about keeping Passover (unleavened bread, dates in Nisan)
- a temple of YHW that Egyptian priests later help destroy (410 BC); the colony writes to Jerusalem and Samaria for help to rebuild

App use: Persian “tolerance” is real and limited. Jews can serve the empire, keep Passover, and still lose a temple to a local riot plus a governor. Esther’s weather is not only Susa. It is also a garrison street.

## 5. Greek and Hasmonean pressure
**Books:** Daniel’s setting-and-reception, 1–2 Maccabees, Sirach, Jubilees.

Archaeology here is broader: gymnasia, coins, Hellenistic cities (Beth-shean/Scythopolis), the desecration remembered in texts more than in a single “pig altar” artifact.

Qumran (separate file of manuscripts already exists) also shows a community’s *daily* kit: many ritual baths, inkwells, dining installations, a calendar fight you can feel in the number of copies of Jubilees. Whether they were “Essenes” is `debated`. The baths and the scrolls are not.

## 6. Galilee and the lake in Jesus’ days
**Books:** Mark, Matthew, Luke; the Capernaum sample day.

### Houses
Capernaum’s first-century rooms are **basalt fieldstone**, small rooms around courtyards, modest. Not mosaic villas. Population of such a village: hundreds to a couple of thousand. Archaeology matches “working town,” not “holy theme park.”

The building long called “Peter’s house” is a courtyard house later honored by Christians (plaster, graffiti, then an octagonal church). That later honor is historical. The nameplate “Simon lived here” is `debated` / tradition. Path can say: *a house like this, in this town.* Summit tells the pilgrimage history.

### Work on the water
- Harbor remains at Capernaum (modest) and **Magdala / Taricheae** (larger: promenade, basin, fish-processing reputation — the Greek name is about salting fish).
- Small finds: net needles, hooks, stone anchors, **lead net-weights**.
- The **Sea of Galilee boat** (1986, mud between Magdala and Capernaum): a first-century working hull, ~8 meters, mixed timbers, dated by pottery and radiocarbon to the late Hellenistic / early Roman window. Popular name “Jesus boat” is marketing. Correct caption: *the size and build of boat these stories assume.*

### Synagogues you can stand in
First-century synagogues are rare and precious:
- **Magdala** (discovered 2009): small, columns, painted plaster, mosaic, coin of AD 43 under a mosaic foundation. The **Magdala Stone** carved with a menorah — a Jewish holy building while the Temple still stood.
- **Gamla**
- Basalt foundations **under** Capernaum’s later white limestone synagogue — a first-century public building is the serious proposal; identifying it as “the” synagogue of Luke 7 is `debated`.

App line: Jesus teaching in synagogues is not an anachronism. The buildings exist. Magdala’s stone is the object-tray piece for “they already gathered to hear Moses.”

### Two rooms on the same lake
Magdala has streets, ritual baths, richer houses, a fish industry.
Capernaum is poorer masonry and a smaller harbor.
That gap is the gospel’s weather: a walking preacher between a processing town and a border village with a toll booth.

## 7. Jerusalem and Rome’s hand
**Books:** Passion week, Acts start, parts of Josephus’ world.

### Jewish daily holiness
Hundreds of **mikva’ot** (ritual baths) in Jerusalem and Judea. Stone vessels everywhere — stone was held not to take ritual impurity, so cups and measuring jars were carved from it. A mikveh sealed under the Western Wall plaza in destruction debris of 70 AD still held ash, pottery, and stone vessels. That is a last ordinary morning under the Temple’s shadow.

### Empire with a name
- **Pilate inscription**, Caesarea Maritima (1961): *Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judea*, on a stone for a Tiberieum. The Gospels’ prefect is not a legend.
- A copper-alloy ring from Herodium inscribed **PILATO** — possibly an official’s seal in Pilate’s administration, not necessarily the prefect’s personal jewelry (`debated`).
- Coins of the prefects and of Tiberius: the object tray for “render to Caesar.”

### A crucified body
Ossuary of **Yehohanan son of Hagkol** (Jerusalem, 1968): a heel bone pierced by an iron nail, wood still attached. A Jewish man in his twenties, crucified by Rome, then buried in the Jewish way. This is the only clear archaeological trace of crucifixion from Jerusalem.

App use: Mark’s cross is not art history. It is a documented local method. Do not claim this bone is any person in the Gospels.

70 AD destruction layers (burned houses in the Upper City, the Burnt House, ash in mikva’ot) are the physical end of the world Revelation and the Gospels can already see coming.

## 8. The Greek street of the letters
**Books:** Paul, Revelation 2–3.

Urban archaeology at **Ephesus, Corinth, Philippi, Laodicea, Smyrna** is rich: shops, terrace houses, latrines, guild spaces, imperial temples, water systems.

For the teaching layer, pick one object per city rather than a virtual tour:
- Corinth: meat-market and temple proximity (the 1 Cor 8 table fight becomes a street).
- Ephesus: Artemis precinct and terrace-house luxury vs. workshop believers.
- Laodicea: water infrastructure that arrives lukewarm after traveling from hot springs and cold sources — a civic fact that makes Revelation 3 sting locally.
- Patmos: a real Aegean island used in Roman punishment geography.

Do not invent a house-church floor plan we do not have. Say: *shops with rooms behind, rented halls, a riverside when there is no building* (Acts 16).

## 9. What archaeology will not do for you

It will not photograph Abraham.
It will not hand you Moses’ brick mold with his name on it.
It will not settle whether Jonah sat under a plant.
It will not turn Magdala’s synagogue into a videotape of Jesus.
It will not replace the text.

What it will do is stop the app from floating.

A grindstone, a quota text, a ration tablet, a net-weight, a mikveh full of ash, a nail in a heel — these are how “empire” becomes a shoulder and a stomach.

## 11. How the World room should cite a find

One block, always:

```
Name of find
Where and when recovered
What it is, in one sentence
How it changes the feeling of this book
What it does not prove
```

Example:

**Lachish Letter 4**  
Gate of Lachish, destruction level, 1935.  
An officer tells his commander they watch Lachish’s fire-signals because Azekah’s can no longer be seen.  
Jeremiah’s last-two-cities line now has dirt under it.  
This is not Jeremiah’s handwriting.

That block is enough. No sermon.
